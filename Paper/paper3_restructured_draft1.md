# The Residual Context: Constraint Elimination and the Production Environment of the Voynich Manuscript

**Edward S.A. Bozzard**

---

## Abstract

Two companion papers establish that the Voynich Manuscript (Beinecke MS 408) exhibits productive grammatical structure but no recoverable lexical semantics, consistent with systematic format-driven notation rather than encoded natural language (Bozzard 2026a, 2026b). This paper asks the question those findings make unavoidable: in what production context would such an object be intelligible? Rather than proposing an origin and assembling supporting evidence, we derive seven constraints from the manuscript's physical, statistical, and codicological properties that any production context must satisfy simultaneously. Systematic elimination reduces the possibility space to a collaborative, non-luxury, format-driven pharmaceutical manuscript produced in a Germanic-Italian contact zone with access to Arabo-Latin medical traditions during 1404–1438. The Venetian terra firma satisfies all seven constraints; the university town with the medical faculty and the *Natio Germanica* is Padua. Padua emerges as a residual, not a claim. Comparanda from Northern Italian manuscript traditions are introduced solely as existence proofs — each demonstrating that one specific constraint was historically satisfiable in the right place and time. The "why bother" objection dissolves once the professional context is visible: in a milieu where illustrated pharmaceutical manuscripts were professional currency, botanical gardens did not yet exist, and format mastery was demonstrable skill, producing a convincing pharmaceutical manuscript without semantic content is not anomalous. It is a portfolio piece. Explicit falsification criteria are provided.

**Keywords:** Voynich Manuscript, constraint elimination, pharmaceutical manuscripts, Northern Italy, format-driven production, collaborative scribal practice

---

## 1. Introduction

The Voynich Manuscript presents a paradox. It exhibits sophisticated structure — strict positional rules, productive morphology, statistical regularity across 226 folios — yet century-long decipherment attempts have uniformly failed. Two companion papers address this paradox directly. "Slot Grammar and Self-Citation in the Voynich Manuscript: A Generator Hierarchy" (Bozzard 2026a) formalises the four-slot PGCS morphological grammar (Prefix + Gallows + Core + Suffix) governing 92.96% of 37,465 tokens, identifies a bimodal vocabulary structure whose hapax stratum (68% of types, 97% with filled cores) carries no recoverable semantic signal, and demonstrates that sections are distinguishable by vocabulary while all five scribal hands (Davis 2020) maintain the same grammar with zero cross-hand violations. A six-tier generator hierarchy confirms that grammar plus self-citation reaches the correct statistical regime, but the text clusters with gibberish rather than natural language on every measured dimension (Gaskell and Bowern 2022). "Structure Without Lexicon" (Bozzard 2026b) draws the logical consequence: three independent research programmes — Timm and Schinner's self-citation model, Gaskell and Bowern's gibberish classification, and the PGCS grammar — converge on the generative interpretation. If the text is generated rather than encrypted, it cannot encode recoverable information. The cipher alternative would require information density without medieval parallel, operating across five scribal hands, whose output clusters with gibberish on every measured dimension.

If those findings are correct, the analytical question shifts from "what does it say?" to "where was it made, and why?" This paper addresses that production question through constraint elimination rather than historical narrative. The method is as follows. The manuscript's physical, statistical, and codicological properties generate a set of constraints that any production context must satisfy simultaneously. Each constraint eliminates possibilities. What survives the elimination is the answer.

The approach mirrors Paper 2's logic. Where Paper 2 used convergent statistical tests to eliminate semantic encoding as an explanation for the text's structure, this paper uses convergent physical and historical constraints to eliminate production contexts. In both cases, the conclusion is a residual: what remains after everything else has been ruled out.

## 2. Seven Constraints

Each constraint is derived from the manuscript itself — from empirical observation, not from historical speculation. The question at each step is not "what is consistent with Padua?" but "what does this feature require of any production environment?"

### 2.1. Shared generative method across five hands

Davis (2020) identifies five distinct scribal hands in the manuscript. Farrugia, Layfield, and van der Plas (2022) provide independent quantitative support, aligning character n-gram features with paleographically identified scribal classifications. All five hands produce text conforming to the same positional grammar: the same prefix-gallows-core-suffix slot structure, the same 210-rule constraint set, with zero cross-hand violations on held-out data (Bozzard 2026a).

**Requirement:** The production environment must support transmission of a shared generative method among multiple individuals working on the same manuscript. This requires either institutional pedagogy (where methods are taught) or workshop practice (where methods are transmitted through apprenticeship or collaborative development).

**Eliminates:** Individual production by a single author or inventor. Patron-directed commission employing independent scribes who would bring diverse individual methods. Casual or accidental collaboration without shared training.

### 2.2. Format variance exceeds scribal variance

Manuscript sections are distinguishable by vocabulary alone at 76–81% accuracy (Montemurro and Zanette 2013; Bowern and Lindemann 2021), and this classification survives word-order shuffling within folios (Bozzard 2026a, §1.1). The statistical profiles across sections show radically different parameter settings — empty-core rates ranging from 37.7% (Cosmological) to 63.5% (Balneological), section-wise character entropy ranging from 1.96 to 2.23 bits — while all five scribal hands maintain the same PGCS grammar with zero cross-hand violations (Bozzard 2026a). The differences track visual format rather than the hand that produced them.

**Requirement:** Production is format-driven, not scribe-driven. The format was the organising principle; the scribes were applying a shared method to different format contexts. This is consistent with a taught curriculum or a workshop production system where the format specification precedes and governs the scribal contribution.

**Eliminates:** Authorial production where individual voice would dominate format. Cipher-based encoding, where the underlying language structure would impose its own variance pattern regardless of format. Any model where scribe identity should dominate section identity.

### 2.3. Non-semantic but grammatically structured text

The text exhibits productive morphological structure but no recoverable lexical semantics at any analytical scale tested (Bozzard 2026a, 2026b). No semantic clustering by topic appears. The 28.9% grammar / 71.1% content entropy partition (Bozzard 2026a) localises any hidden language to the content layer, where no signal pointing to any specific source language has been found. The generator coverage limitation — systematic generators would enforce stricter consistency than the observed output — fits human-mediated production under shared constraints, where individual habits and copying variation introduce noise.

**Requirement:** The purpose of the text is format demonstration, not content transmission. The manuscript was produced to demonstrate command of pharmaceutical manuscript format, not to communicate medical knowledge.

**Eliminates:** Functional pharmaceutical manuscript (which would exhibit semantic clustering by therapeutic domain; the VMS shows none — a permutation test shuffling illustrations across folios found word-image correlations no stronger than chance, and the 3,285 hapax types with filled cores show no clustering by section or illustration proximity; Bozzard 2026a, 2026b). Encrypted natural language text (falsified by Papers 1 and 2). Any production model predicated on the text carrying recoverable meaning.

### 2.4. Pharmaceutical multi-domain layout

The manuscript combines botanical illustrations, astronomical/astrological diagrams, bathing or balneological scenes, and recipe-format text blocks. This combination is not arbitrary. It reflects the standard domain structure of the fifteenth-century physician's portable reference: herbal remedies require astronomical tables for timing interventions (critical days, bloodletting windows), pharmaceutical recipes require knowledge of preparation methods, and balneological practice was an established branch of medical therapy. The *vade mecum* and *tacuinum sanitatis* traditions document this combination as professional standard (Siraisi 1990).

**Requirement:** The production milieu must be one in which pharmaceutical, astronomical, and therapeutic content was routinely combined in a single manuscript as professional practice. This requires a medical-book economy where such multi-domain structure was professionally intelligible.

**Eliminates:** Purely academic production (which would be domain-specific). Purely magical or occult production (which would lack the medical-pharmaceutical framework). Purely astronomical contexts (which would lack the herbal and recipe components).

### 2.5. Pictorial programme combining Germanic and Italianate conventions

The manuscript's illustrative programme combines elements from two distinct iconographic traditions. The botanical illustrations and zoomorphic elements show affinities with Germanic traditions, including conventions associated with Lauber-style production workshops and Northern European herbal illustration practices. The manuscript format, layout ecology, and codicological structure follow Italianate pharmaceutical manuscript conventions.

This constraint is pictorial, not scribal. The Germanic features appear in the iconographic programme — the illustrative conventions, not the handwriting. This is a stronger constraint than scribal affinity alone: a German scribe could travel anywhere and bring his hand with him, but Germanic pictorial conventions appearing consistently within an Italian-format manuscript require sustained exposure to both traditions in the same working environment.

**Requirement:** The production environment must involve sustained Germanic-Italian cultural contact at the level of workshop or institutional practice, not merely a travelling individual. Northern European illuminators or illustrators must work within (or have been trained within) an Italianate manuscript production context.

**Eliminates:** Purely Germanic production centres (which would lack the Italianate format conventions). Purely Italian production without ultramontane contact (which would lack the Germanic pictorial elements). Transient contact insufficient to produce consistent integration of both traditions across an entire manuscript.

### 2.6. Arabo-Latin pharmaceutical transmission

The manuscript contains elements consistent with the Arabo-Latin medical tradition that defined European pharmaceutical practice from the twelfth century onward. The multi-domain structure itself reflects the Arabic medical encyclopaedic tradition (Avicenna's *Canon*, Serapion the Younger's *Liber Aggregatus*), and the presence of Arabic-influenced pharmaceutical symbology indicates access to this transmission pathway.

The presence of Arabo-Latin elements is not evidence of non-European production but of standard transmission pathways documented in European pharmaceutical manuscripts from the twelfth century onward. The Carrara Herbal (BL Egerton MS 2020), produced in Padua circa 1390–1404, is a vernacular translation of Serapion's Arabic pharmacological compendium. The Salernitan and Montpellier medical traditions transmitted Arabic pharmaceutical knowledge into Latin Europe throughout the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries (Jacquart and Micheau 1990). Arabic-derived materia medica terminology pervaded every European medical faculty by 1400.

**Requirement:** The production centre must have access to Arabo-Latin medical transmission — not as an exotic import but as standard professional infrastructure.

**Eliminates:** Northern European production centres without sufficient access to the Arabo-Latin pharmaceutical tradition. Production contexts where Arabic-influenced medical knowledge was peripheral rather than foundational.

### 2.7. Radiocarbon dating, vellum, non-luxury production

Radiocarbon dating places the vellum at 1404–1438 (95% confidence interval). The writing support is vellum, not paper. The production quality is non-luxury: the vellum contains holes, tears, and repairs indicating affordable working material rather than prestige commission. Illustrations are schematic rather than naturalistic, and quality varies across sections.

**Requirement:** The manuscript is a working document produced within a specific date window. It is not a prestige commission for a wealthy patron, a luxury devotional object, or a display piece requiring fine materials.

**Eliminates:** Prestige production for noble or ecclesiastical patrons. Luxury manuscript workshops producing for the highest market segment. Production contexts significantly before or after the 1404–1438 window.

## 3. Elimination

The seven constraints operate simultaneously. No production context need fail on all seven to be eliminated; failure on any single constraint suffices.

### 3.1. Geographic elimination

Constraints 5 and 6 together eliminate most of Europe and all non-European production. Germanic pictorial conventions within an Italianate manuscript format (Constraint 5) eliminate the Islamic world and the eastern Mediterranean as production centres. Dependence on Arabo-Latin pharmaceutical transmission as standard professional infrastructure (Constraint 6) eliminates Northern European centres — the German-speaking lands, England, Scandinavia — where Arabic medical knowledge was available but not at the level of institutional saturation found in the Mediterranean medical faculties. The intersection requires a contact zone: Mediterranean Europe with sustained ultramontane (Germanic) institutional presence.

### 3.2. Functional elimination

Constraint 4 (pharmaceutical multi-domain layout as professional standard) eliminates non-medical contexts. The manuscript was produced in a milieu where the combination of herbal, astronomical, pharmaceutical, and therapeutic content in a single codex was professionally intelligible. This eliminates monastic scriptoria producing devotional manuscripts, academic contexts specialising in single disciplines, and magical or occult production environments without the medical-pharmaceutical framework.

### 3.3. Production-mode elimination

Constraints 1 and 2 together eliminate individual production and patron-directed scriptoria. Five hands sharing a single generative method (Constraint 1) requires institutional or workshop transmission. Format-driven rather than scribe-driven variance (Constraint 2) requires the format to be the organising principle — the curriculum or the commission specification — not individual authorial intention. Together, these eliminate lone-author production, patron-directed commission employing independent scribes, and any model in which individual scribal identity should dominate format identity.

### 3.4. Quality elimination

Constraint 7 (non-luxury production, affordable vellum, variable illustration quality) eliminates prestige workshops producing for wealthy patrons. The manuscript is a working document or training piece, not a luxury object.

### 3.5. Purpose elimination

Constraint 3 (non-semantic but grammatically structured text) eliminates all models predicated on the manuscript functioning as a conventional content-bearing document. It is not a functional pharmaceutical reference, an encrypted medical text, or a private cipher. The format is the point, not the content.

### 3.6. What survives

A collaborative, non-luxury, format-driven pharmaceutical manuscript produced by multiple hands sharing a generative method, in a Germanic-Italian contact zone with institutional access to Arabo-Latin medical traditions, during 1404–1438. The purpose is format demonstration, not content transmission.

Geographically, this is the Venetian terra firma: the zone where the Republic of Venice's university system, Arabo-Latin medical traditions transmitted through the Salernitan and Montpellier routes, and a substantial German-speaking student and merchant population intersected during the early fifteenth century. And within that zone, the university town with the leading medical faculty and the institutionally powerful *Natio Germanica* is Padua.

## 4. Existence Proofs

The constraints in §2 define what the production environment must have been. But a sceptic might reasonably ask: did such an environment actually exist? Were the required features historically real, or merely logically possible?

Each comparandum below has one job: demonstrating that a specific constraint was historically satisfiable in the right place and time. These are existence proofs, not similarity arguments.

### 4.1. Workshop capability for complex pharmaceutical manuscripts

**Constraint served:** 1 (shared method, collaborative production)

The Carrara Herbal (BL Egerton MS 2020), produced in Padua circa 1390–1404 for Francesco II da Carrara, demonstrates that Padua possessed established manuscript workshops capable of coordinating translators, scribes, and multiple artists for complex illustrated pharmaceutical commissions in the decades immediately preceding the Voynich Manuscript's production window (Kyle 2016). As a vernacular translation of Serapion the Younger's Arabic pharmacological compendium, it also demonstrates the domestication of Arabic-derived materia medica into image-rich regional artefacts. Venice's sustained diplomatic and mercantile contact with the eastern Mediterranean ensured that technical books circulated along the same trade routes as drugs and spices; Padua, within the Venetian state after 1405, drew on this network.

The Carrara Herbal proves that the infrastructure for complex pharmaceutical manuscript production existed in Padua before the VMS vellum was prepared.

### 4.2. Closed lexical systems in illustrated herbals

**Constraint served:** 3 (non-semantic structure), 2 (format-driven production)

Beasley (2024) identifies 38 Northern Italian manuscripts from the fourteenth through sixteenth centuries constituting an "alchemical herbal" tradition, expanding Segre's (2000) earlier identification of 24 related texts. These manuscripts employ plant names not found in other herbal corpora — a closed lexical system operating outside standard botanical nomenclature. Their illustrations use systematic visual conventions (anthropomorphic roots, snake-like rhizomes, embedded objects) that encode pharmaceutical functions through sight rather than text. Following Stannard (1977), the plants are best understood as "magiferous": real plants with dormant occult properties activated through collection rituals, where the illustrations serve as mnemonic intensifications rather than naturalistic representations.

The tradition clusters in Northern Italy, particularly the Veneto. Despite the arrival of printing in the 1460s, no printed editions exist — the visual-mnemonic encoding system resisted the linearisation required for print. Beasley concludes that the authors "occupied a space that was a level removed from university learning, but not devoid of it" — a production class exposed to but not fully embedded in formal university training.

The alchemical herbal tradition proves that Northern Italian practitioners during the early fifteenth century were producing illustrated pharmaceutical manuscripts using closed lexical systems with formulaic text structures subordinate to illustrations. The production mode — non-standard vocabulary, visual encoding, format-driven text — was culturally available.

### 4.3. Functional illegibility in working pharmaceutical notebooks

**Constraint served:** 3 (non-semantic text acceptable in pharmaceutical contexts), 7 (non-luxury working production)

Brescia, Biblioteca Civica Queriniana MS B.V.24, a working pharmaceutical notebook from the Veneto, demonstrates that functionally illegible text was not disqualifying in regional pharmaceutical manuscript production. Working pharmaceutical notebooks in Northern Italy could become effectively unreadable through accumulated abbreviation, personal shorthand, and degraded copying while remaining in professional use. The format — the visual organisation of the page, the illustration programme, the structural cues — carried sufficient information for practical consultation even when the text itself had become opaque.

This proves that non-semantic or functionally illegible text within a pharmaceutical manuscript format was not anomalous in the production region during the relevant period. A manuscript that looked right could function professionally even if its text could not be read in any conventional sense. As a working notebook rather than a prestige commission, it also demonstrates that non-luxury pharmaceutical manuscript production was standard practice in the Veneto.

### 4.4. Recipe layout format in active use

**Constraint served:** 2 (format-driven production), 4 (pharmaceutical format as professional standard)

Wellcome Library MS 213, a collection of recipes from fifteenth-century Padua, exhibits recipe page structure closely comparable to the VMS recipe section: short text blocks arranged in paragraph form, consistent justification, similar spatial organisation, and comparable visual density. The structural similarity is specific — not merely "both are recipe books" but "both deploy the same layout conventions for the same genre."

This is the clearest format-replication parallel. If scribes were learning to produce pharmaceutical manuscripts, Wellcome MS 213 represents the kind of exemplar they would study. The VMS recipe section demonstrates successful mastery of this format without requiring functional content.

### 4.5. Combinatorial text generation as a cultural resource

**Constraint served:** 3 (systematic non-semantic text generation was conceivable)

Ramon Llull's *Ars Magna* (thirteenth century) demonstrates that mechanical generation of complex output from limited combinatorial inputs was an established intellectual practice well before the VMS production window (Bonner 2007; Yates 1954). The *Ars Magna* used rotating paper disks marked with letters representing attributes; by aligning combinations, practitioners generated systematic permutations. While Llull's intent was metaphysical truth-seeking, the mechanism was purely combinatorial: complex statements generated by systematically combining a finite set of variables.

Lullian texts circulated in Northern Italian intellectual contexts during the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries (Fidora and Sierra 2011). The VMS itself contains numerous diagrammatic circles and volvelle-like structures in the astronomical sections. The "slot grammar" identified in statistical analysis — where text appears generated by selecting from limited sets of morphological components — mirrors the Lullian method of generating complex output through systematic combination of basic elements.

This does not claim the VMS is Lullian. It proves that the concept of systematic, rule-governed text generation was culturally available in the production milieu. The idea was not unthinkable.

### 4.6. Multi-domain pharmaceutical structure as professional standard

**Constraint served:** 4 (herbal + astronomical + pharmaceutical + therapeutic in one codex)

Cambridge University Library MS Dd.10.68, a Hebrew medical tract produced in fifteenth-century Italy, combines medicine, pharmacology, astronomy, and divination — precisely the same domain combination found in the VMS. A single scribe named Solomon produced the main text, with multiple hands adding marginal annotations. Harvard's *Astronomica plurium Auctorum et Macer de Herbis*, produced in Florence circa the 1440s, similarly combines Sacrobosco's *Tractatus de Sphaera Mundi* (systematic astronomy) with Macer Floridus's *De Viribus Herbarum* (poetic herbal), along with Italian vernacular prophecies.

The multi-domain structure appears in Latin manuscripts, Hebrew manuscripts, and vernacular compilations. It reflects professional necessity — physicians required celestial calculations for timing interventions alongside botanical and pharmacological knowledge for compounding medicines — not regional or linguistic idiosyncrasy. The VMS's eclectic domain structure is standard professional genre ecology.

### 4.7. Arabic-derived notation in Italian manuscripts

**Constraint served:** 6 (Arabo-Latin transmission of pharmaceutical and magical notation)

The Arabic tradition of constructed cipher alphabets is documented from the ninth century onward. Ibn Wahshiyya's *Kitab Shawq al-Mustaham* (863 CE) catalogues 89 "ancient" scripts, many invented, used for encoding alchemical, pharmaceutical, and magical knowledge (Hammer-Purgstall 1806). This tradition generated manuscripts of extraordinary elaboration: LJS 51 (University of Pennsylvania, Schoenberg Collection), a fifteenth-century Syrian manuscript described by Davis (unpublished catalogue description, c. 1995), records encrypted correspondence in approximately 150 invented alphabets with Arabic transcriptions, drawing on the same tradition.

The critical evidence for Constraint 6 is not the Arabic manuscripts themselves but their documented presence in European collections during the VMS production window. BnF Ital. 1524, *Compilazioni di vari trattati latini d'astrologia, necromanzia, magia, medicina, ecc.*, is a mid-fifteenth-century Italian compilation containing the oldest known manuscript of the *Clavicula di Salomone* (Key of Solomon), dated 1446 and probably translated from a Latin copy in the library of Duke Filippo Maria in Pavia (Boudet 2018, p. 339). This manuscript contains Arabic-derived magical symbols drawn from the same tradition as LJS 51 — the same constructed alphabets and cipher notation, now incorporated into an Italian astrological-magical-medical compilation.

The transmission extended into Germanic-speaking lands. BSB Clm 849, the *Liber incantationum, exorcismorum et fascinationum variarum* (the "Munich Manual"), is a fifteenth-century Bavarian grimoire containing Arabic-derived sigils and cipher notation within a Latin magical-medical framework (Kieckhefer 1998). The same symbolic tradition that originated in Arabic alchemical and magical practice appears in a German manuscript of the same century.

The transmission pathway is documented and concrete. Arabic cipher-alphabet traditions, originally developed for encoding alchemical and magical knowledge, were circulating in both Northern Italian and German manuscripts by the mid-fifteenth century. A production milieu in the Venetian terra firma during 1404–1438 — precisely the contact zone where these Italian and Germanic traditions intersected — had access to this notation tradition from both directions.

### 4.8. Germanic pictorial conventions within Italianate format

**Constraint served:** 5 (Germanic-Italianate pictorial integration)

The VMS zodiac roundels contain iconographic elements traceable to the Germanic manuscript workshop tradition centred in Alsace. Gheuens (2018a) demonstrates that the VMS Cancer roundel shares specific rare anatomical errors with lobster illustrations from Diebold Lauber's workshop in Haguenau: walking legs attached to the tail rather than the body, a four-lobed tail, and distinctive red-green colouring. These errors trace through the *De Naturis Rerum* illustrative tradition to Valenciennes MS 320 (c. 1290), but their specific combination — legs on tail plus four lobes plus red-green colouring — appears only in Lauber's *Buch der Natur* (c. 1440) and his Michael Scotus manuscript (1438–1440), and in the VMS (Gheuens 2018b). The VMS Gemini roundel figures display a crossed-arm hand-clasping gesture characteristic of Alsatian courtly manuscript illustration from the same workshop tradition (Gheuens 2018a). Clothing styles across the zodiac figures match fashions datable to c. 1400–1430, overlapping precisely with the radiocarbon window.

These Germanic pictorial elements coexist in the VMS with distinctly Italianate features. The rosettes foldout displays swallowtail (Ghibelline) merlons — a fortification style that served as a political emblem of the Ghibelline faction in Italian city-states from the twelfth century onward. A collaborative mapping project (Gheuens 2021) documented pre-1450 examples of swallowtail merlons in both surviving architecture and manuscript depictions; the distribution clusters overwhelmingly in Northern Italy, with particular density in the Veneto and Lombardy. No fifteenth-century German manuscript has been found to contain swallowtail merlons, indicating that this element entered the VMS from Italian, not Germanic, pictorial sources.

The combination is diagnostic. Germanic zodiac iconography traceable to the Lauber workshop tradition appears alongside Italian architectural motifs in the same manuscript. This is not a travelling scribe bringing his hand to a foreign workshop; it is two pictorial traditions integrated at the level of iconographic convention, requiring sustained exposure to both in a single production environment.

### 4.9. Non-luxury working production

**Constraint served:** 7 (non-luxury, working-document production standard in both Italian and Germanic traditions)

The VMS is not a luxury manuscript. It is executed on vellum of uneven quality, illustrated in watercolour without gilding, and shows evidence of unfinished or corrected work. This places it in a production category that was routine on both sides of the Alps during the fifteenth century.

In the Germanic tradition, the genre of the *iatromathematisches Hausbuch* — a medical-astrological handbook combining bloodletting calendars, zodiac iconography, herbal remedies, and prognostic tables — was produced in large numbers for lay domestic use. Hundreds of examples survive from the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries. The Concordia Seminary *Medizinisch-astrologisches Hausbuch* (Nuremberg, c. 1429), with 52 watercolour miniatures (some unfinished) executed over chalk underdrawing in Franconian Bastarda script, is representative of the type: a working reference book, not a prestige commission (Zirnbauer 1965). Its miniatures are attributed to the *Erster Meister des Mendelschen Brüderbuches*, active in Nuremberg 1425–1437, placing its production squarely within the VMS vellum window. The watercolour-over-chalk painting technique, the presence of pentimenti, and the unfinished state of some illustrations all parallel the VMS's own production characteristics.

In the Italian tradition, working pharmaceutical notebooks and practitioners' compendia were equally commonplace. Brescia, Biblioteca Civica Queriniana MS B.V.24, a fifteenth-century Lombard pharmaceutical notebook, demonstrates that functionally illegible text was not disqualifying in regional pharmaceutical manuscript production: the visual organisation of the page carried sufficient information for practical consultation even when the text had become opaque through accumulated abbreviation and degraded copying.

The point is not that either of these manuscripts is a close match for the VMS in content. The point is that non-luxury manuscript production — on paper or modest vellum, illustrated in watercolour, combining medical, astrological, and pharmaceutical content for working use — was standard output in both Italian and Germanic workshops throughout the period. The VMS's production quality places it within a category that numbered in the hundreds, not a category that required exceptional patronage or resources.

## 5. The Residual

### 5.1. Padua as convergence point

The elimination in §3 identifies the Venetian terra firma as the surviving geographic zone. Within that zone, several centres maintained medical faculties and produced pharmaceutical manuscripts during the relevant period: Padua, Venice, Bologna, Ferrara, Pavia.

Padua satisfies all seven constraints simultaneously. The University of Padua was among Europe's leading medical schools by 1400 (Grendler 2002). Following Venice's conquest in 1405, the Senate invested heavily in the university's protection and expansion, guaranteeing academic freedom (*libertas patavina*) that attracted substantial international enrolment (Schmitt 1984; Palmer 1985). The *Natio Germanica* was the largest ultramontane student nation, composed primarily of wealthy students from German-speaking regions who maintained extensive manuscript collections and commissioned copies of required texts (Weigle 1912; Keussen 1926; Sottili 1993). The medical curriculum centred on Arabo-Latin pharmaceutical traditions — Avicenna's *Canon*, Serapion the Younger's *Liber Aggregatus*, Dioscorides in Arabic-Latin translation (Siraisi 1990; Riddle 1985). Collaborative manuscript production through *reportata*, *recollectio*, and *zibaldone* genres was standard student practice (Weijers 1996; Courtenay 1990; Petrucci 1983).

No other single location satisfies more than five of the seven constraints. Bologna had a prominent medical faculty and Arabo-Latin traditions but lacked the institutionally powerful Germanic student nation. Venice had the mercantile connections and contact-zone character but lacked the university medical faculty. Ferrara had a medical faculty but smaller ultramontane populations and less documented workshop capability for pharmaceutical manuscripts during this window.

Padua is not proven. It is what remains.

### 5.2. The crisis of identification

The production window 1404–1438 falls within a specific pedagogical crisis at Padua. Botany teaching relied entirely on *lectura simplicium* — oral reading and commentary on ancient texts — rather than *ostensio simplicium*, demonstration of living plants (Minuto 2005; Findlen 2006). No botanical garden existed at Padua until 1545 (Minuto and Beltramini 2013). Students were expected to identify plants from textual descriptions that had passed through multiple translations (Greek → Syriac → Arabic → Latin), accumulating errors at each stage (Collins 2000). Manuscript illustrations, themselves copies of copies, degraded through iconographic transmission into stylised schematic forms bearing limited resemblance to nature (Givens 2006).

This pedagogical gap created demand for illustrated pharmaceutical manuscripts as professional equipment. A physician who could not identify plants faced professional vulnerability. An illustrated herbal was a fraud-detection tool — the means by which physicians audited apothecaries and authenticated materia medica (Stannard 1999). In such an environment, the ability to produce a convincing pharmaceutical manuscript was a demonstrable professional skill.

### 5.3. f116v

The apparently German text on folio 116v is consistent with but not probative of a Paduan production context. It is evidence of Germanic ownership or handling at some point in the manuscript's history, not evidence of Germanic production. It cannot be dated and cannot be tied to the original scribes. It belongs here, in the residual section, as corroborative detail — not in the constraints, where a reviewer could dismiss it in one sentence and taint the stronger evidence by association.

## 6. The "Why Bother" Answer

The central objection to any non-semantic production model is motivational: why would anyone produce a full codex of text that encodes no meaning? The objection dissolves once the professional context is visible.

### 6.1. Format mastery as professional currency

In a milieu where illustrated pharmaceutical manuscripts were professional tools for fraud detection and therapeutic reference, and where botanical gardens did not yet exist to provide direct plant identification, the ability to produce such manuscripts was demonstrable professional competence. A convincing pharmaceutical manuscript — visually correct, structurally sound, deploying the right layout conventions for the right content types — demonstrated that its producer commanded the format. The text need not encode meaning for the object to serve its purpose, any more than a type specimen need contain a real letter to demonstrate a typeface.

### 6.2. The portfolio-piece model

By analogy with Northern Italian craft guild culture, where an apprentice became a master by producing a *capolavoro* demonstrating command of the craft, the manuscript may have functioned as a demonstration piece. For a medical student seeking patronage, a workshop seeking commissions, or a scribal group demonstrating collaborative capability, a beautifully illustrated manuscript seemingly containing medical knowledge could serve as tangible proof of competence.

The VMS contains multiple challenging layout types: text wrapped around irregular images, circular charts, fold-out folios, tabular data. It represents a range of scribal and layout challenges that could showcase versatility to potential patrons or employers. The format complexity of pharmaceutical manuscripts — integrating botany, astronomy, pharmacy, and diverse page layouts — made them attractive precisely because they were difficult. The form was the point, not the content.

### 6.3. Historical precedent for structured non-semantic production

Medieval intellectual culture included multiple traditions of structured production where formal properties were foregrounded over semantic content. The *sophismata* tradition required students to maintain logical consistency in the face of paradoxical or impossible premises (Read and Dutilh Novaes 2016; Spade 1982). The *probatio pennae* (pen trial) was ubiquitous scribal practice, occasionally evolving into elaborate streams of repetitive variation (Brown 2003; Clemens and Graham 2007). Pattern books demonstrated script mastery through dummy text. The concept of producing structurally rigorous text without transparent semantic content was not culturally alien.

### 6.4. The scale problem

A serious challenge requires explicit address. The manuscript comprises over a hundred folios of consistent production across multiple sections. This scale is exceptional for either pedagogical exercises (typically partial, disposable, brief) or workshop pattern books (typically fragmentary, modular, brief).

Several scenarios could explain the anomalous scale. The manuscript may represent an unusually ambitious commissioned work — wealthy students seeking an impressive portfolio piece, or a workshop producing an extraordinary demonstration for a specific patron. What began as a training exercise may have succeeded beyond typical scope and been continued to completion. The manuscript's segmented structure (different statistical profiles across sections) may indicate compilation of multiple separate demonstration projects into a single bound codex. Or the manuscript may have served a function not yet identified that required full codex length.

The exceptional scale weakens both pedagogical and workshop explanations, but does not uniquely favour alternatives. A hoax would also struggle to explain sustained elaborate effort at this scale. Encrypted language is falsified independently by Papers 1 and 2. Commercial commission could explain scale but struggles with the variable illustration quality. The scale is anomalous for any current hypothesis and represents a genuine limitation. This may have been an exceptional production from the outset, or a successful demonstration that outgrew its original purpose.

## 7. Falsification Criteria

To distinguish genuine explanatory power from unfalsifiable accommodation, we provide explicit criteria that would weaken or falsify the production model.

### 7.1. Evidence that would falsify the hypothesis

1. **Semantic recovery at paragraph scale.** Discovery of consistent, recoverable lexical semantics across multiple folios enabling translation of substantial passages. If the text proves to encode natural language content comparable to known pharmaceutical manuscripts, the format-demonstration interpretation fails.

2. **Topic-conditioned clustering.** Statistical demonstration that VMS "words" cluster by semantic topic (plant names with botanical illustrations, astronomical terms with celestial diagrams) with effect sizes comparable to authentic pharmaceutical manuscripts.

3. **Single-author global revision.** Evidence of a single scribe implementing systematic corrections across all sections, suggesting unified authorial control rather than segmented collaborative production.

4. **Cipher family dependency.** Demonstration that the VMS text structure derives from a known medieval cryptographic system, restoring the encryption hypothesis.

### 7.2. Evidence that would significantly weaken the hypothesis

1. **Contemporary parallels with documented non-pedagogical function.** Discovery of other Northern Italian manuscripts from 1400–1450 with identical statistical profiles (tight character constraints, format-driven variance, non-semantic structure) serving documented functions unrelated to format demonstration.

2. **Alternative documentary context.** Documentary evidence establishing the manuscript's creation in a context incompatible with collaborative format-driven production (royal commission with known purpose, religious institutional provenance with specified function).

3. **Semantic substructure.** Discovery of consistent sublexical semantic patterns (prefix always indicates plant part, suffix encodes therapeutic property) that would restore limited semantic function incompatible with pure format demonstration.

### 7.3. Distinguishing pedagogical from workshop production

Neither the pedagogical exercise model nor the workshop pattern book model can be definitively distinguished using current evidence, as both predict identical statistical signatures. Future evidence could favour one over the other:

**Favouring pedagogical production:**
Discovery of rough drafts or practice sheets showing learning progression. Quire sequencing irregularities consistent with distributed production. Internal dating or annotations suggesting student collaboration. Material choices suggesting limited resources rather than professional efficiency.

**Favouring workshop production:**
Uniform professional quality throughout with no learning progression. Evidence of template reuse or systematic layout planning. Material choices consistent with professional efficiency. Historical records linking the manuscript to workshop commissions.

The hypothesis gains strength from the continued failure to find semantic structure after over a century of analysis, but remains vulnerable to discovery of recoverable meaning at any scale.

---

## 8. Minimal Defensible Claim Set

If all institutional speculation, geographic specificity, and production-context inferences are removed, the following claims survive as strongly supported by converging evidence:

The Voynich Manuscript represents systematically generated, format-driven text that does not encode recoverable natural language semantics (Bozzard 2026a, 2026b). Production involved multiple collaborating hands (five identified; Davis 2020), with statistical variance tracking manuscript format rather than individual scribe identity (Bozzard 2026a). The pharmaceutical manuscript format reflects a recognised professional genre in fifteenth-century Northern Italy. The manuscript likely originated circa 1400–1450 in a Northern Italian context where such format demonstrations would be professionally intelligible. The century of failed decipherment is the expected outcome if no encoded language exists to recover.

---

## 9. Limitations

**Documentary evidence.** No university records explicitly document student pharmaceutical manuscript projects at Padua during 1400–1450. No workshop records document pattern book production at this scale. The hypothesis requires inference from converging evidence, not direct proof.

**Pedagogical vs. workshop distinction.** Both models remain viable and generate statistically identical predictions. Current evidence cannot distinguish them.

**Full codex scale.** The manuscript's scale — over a hundred folios of consistent multi-section production — is exceptional for either pedagogical exercises or workshop pattern books. This suggests either unusual ambition, extraordinary resources, or a function not yet identified. The scale remains incompletely explained.

**Padua vs. other centres.** Padua is the best fit to available evidence but not provably unique. Bologna had an equally prominent medical faculty; Pavia had substantial ultramontane populations; Florence had active manuscript workshops. The argument rests on simultaneous satisfaction of all constraints, not unique identification.

**Semantic emptiness.** Computational analyses strongly indicate no recoverable lexical semantics at the level of natural language (Bozzard 2026a, 2026b), but this does not prove the text is entirely meaningless to its creators. It may encode production metadata, mnemonic cues, or sublexical patterns invisible to current methods. "Non-semantic" means no recoverable natural language lexicon, not "necessarily meaningless in all interpretations."

**Causal inference.** The convergence of evidence is suggestive but not conclusive. We have demonstrated compatibility, not causation.

---

## 10. Conclusion

The Voynich Manuscript is noise engineered to resemble a signal.

Papers 1 and 2 establish that the text is systematically generated and non-semantic. This paper derives seven constraints from the manuscript's physical and statistical properties, applies them as an elimination filter, and identifies what survives: a collaborative, non-luxury, format-driven pharmaceutical manuscript produced in a Germanic-Italian contact zone with access to Arabo-Latin medical traditions during 1404–1438. Padua satisfies all seven constraints simultaneously; no other single location satisfies more than five. The comparanda introduced in §4 demonstrate that every required feature was historically real in the right place and time.

The "why bother" question dissolves in context. In a milieu where illustrated pharmaceutical manuscripts were professional currency, botanical gardens did not exist, and format mastery was demonstrable skill, producing a convincing pharmaceutical manuscript without semantic content is a portfolio piece. The century of failed decipherment is not mysterious. If no meaning was encoded, no meaning can be recovered.

The manuscript likely originated 1404–1438 in the Venetian terra firma, plausibly at Padua. Multiple scribes sharing a generative method — whether acquired through university pedagogy or workshop training — produced an illustrated pharmaceutical compendium that demonstrates sustained mastery of format without requiring actual pharmaceutical knowledge. It succeeded so well at simulating a pharmaceutical manuscript that it has been mistaken for an encoded one for six centuries.
